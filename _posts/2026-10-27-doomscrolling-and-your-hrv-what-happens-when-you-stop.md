Doomscrolling has a reputation as a villain for your nervous system. So here's an experiment that should have proved it.

Austrian researchers asked 111 students to cut their total smartphone time - not just the doom - to two hours a day for three weeks, down from an average of about four and a half hours. The expected result showed up: less stress, fewer depressive symptoms, better sleep and well-being, all small to medium in size ([Pieh et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z)). Then a follow-up analysis checked overnight [HRV](/blog/how-to-interpret-apple-watch-heart-rate-variability) from the students' Fitbits, and the unexpected result showed up. It had dipped during the cutback ([Dale et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2025.2546376)).

Feeling better, HRV lower. If the phone was straining their nervous systems, putting it down should have calmed them. The likely explanation says something useful about what scrolling does to your body, and about how to read your own numbers.

## Why Does Your Heart Rate Slow Down While You Scroll?

Start with what scrolling itself does. A Durham University team connected 54 regular Instagram users to a chest ECG and finger sweat sensors, then ran three 15-minute phases: reading a neutral news article, scrolling Instagram, and going back to reading ([Wadsley & Ihssen, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108597)).

During Instagram, heart rate dropped about three beats per minute below the reading baseline while the sweat response rose markedly. A slowing heart with sweaty fingertips isn't the classic stress signature, which is a racing heart. The researchers read the combination as absorbed, reward-driven attention.

The stress-like part came at the end. When participants had to stop and go back to reading, with Instagram notifications arriving on a phone they weren't allowed to touch, heart rate ticked back up and the sweat response rose further. They also rated themselves as more stressed, more anxious, and more eager to check the app than when they started. The physiological pattern looked the same in people with high and low problematic-use scores.

It was a small, student sample, so treat it as a clue. But it flips the usual story. The scroll is absorbing. The stop is what feels like stress. None of that is a character flaw - it's a nervous system responding to something rewarding.

## What Changes When the Feed Is Bad News?

Instagram isn't doom. Add threat, and the body notices.

In a cross-national experiment, about 1,100 people in 17 countries watched BBC World News clips with sensors on their fingers. On average, negative stories drew a bigger sweat response than positive ones, along with higher heart rate variability during the clip, a pattern that fits heightened attention and arousal ([Soroka et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1908369116)). The effect varied a lot between people, and not every country showed it.

A note for HRV watchers: a higher reading during a tense clip doesn't mean calm. It's a snapshot of reactivity, and it answers a different question than the overnight recovery number your wearable reports.

Dose matters too. After the 2013 Boston Marathon bombings, researchers surveyed 4,675 US adults. People who reported six or more hours a day of bombing-related media in the following week had more acute stress symptoms than people who had actually been at or near the bombings, even after adjusting for prior mental health ([Holman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1316265110)). That's self-reported, correlational, and an extreme dose. The direction is still hard to ignore.

And it loops. A three-year follow-up found that heavy bombing coverage predicted stress symptoms months later, those symptoms predicted more worry about future events, and that worry predicted more media use, and more stress, when the next tragedy hit a year later ([Thompson et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6469939)). Distress drives the scroll, and the scroll feeds the distress. That's the "doom" in doomscrolling.

Both studies are about coverage of mass-violence events, not feeds in general. They're still the best data we have on dose and loop.

## Does Doomscrolling Actually Lower Your HRV?

Back to the Austrian trial. The wearable analysis covered the 45 students with complete HRV data, so it's an early clue, not a verdict. Still, overnight HRV was significantly lower during the cutback than at baseline, and in exploratory analyses it tracked two things: how much people craved their phones, and how well they slept ([Dale et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2025.2546376)). The authors suggest a response akin to withdrawal: take away a rewarding habit, and your body can register a stressor even while you report feeling better. They also note that the mental-health gains outweighed the cost.

That fits the lab data: scrolling is absorbing, and stopping is the arousing part. It's also a clean example of HRV and mood pointing in opposite directions, the gap we unpack in [why your stress level doesn't match how you feel](/blog/why-your-stress-level-doesn-t-match-how-you-feel). And because craving and sleep quality both moved with HRV, your [overnight sleep data](/blog/what-your-sleep-data-really-means-for-stress) is worth reading alongside it.

So, does doomscrolling lower your HRV? Honestly, nobody has shown it directly. These studies measured heart rate, sweat response, symptoms, and HRV during a cutback. None tracked what a night of doomscrolling does to tomorrow morning's number. It's a reasonable hypothesis, not an established fact.

## How to Scroll Less Without Fighting Your Nervous System

- **Plan the exit, not just the limit.** In the lab, stopping was the hard part. Decide what happens in the first two minutes after you put the phone down: stand up, change rooms, start the kettle. If you still feel wired, slow paced breathing is a well-studied way to nudge your body toward calm - [here's which pattern fits when](/blog/box-breathing-vs-resonance-vs-4-7-8-which-helps-your-hrv). (The Instagram study didn't test fixes, so this is a logical next step, not a proven one.)
- **Cap the doom, not just the minutes.** The strongest links to stress involved heavy, repeated exposure to coverage of bad events. Choose one or two news check-ins a day, and notice when the urge to check rises with your worry. That's the loop at work.
- **Expect a rough patch.** If you cut back and feel restless, or your wearable looks worse for a while, that matches what the trial saw. Judge it over weeks, with sleep and mood alongside, not off one morning. And plan for drift: by follow-up, the cutback group's screen time had crept back toward where it started ([Pieh et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z)).
- **Run your own test.** Compare overnight HRV and sleep after evenings you scrolled late with evenings you didn't, against [your own baseline](/blog/what-your-wearable-s-hrv-number-actually-means). Keep alcohol and caffeine steady so you're testing the phone, not the nightcap.

Tonight's experiment: pick a stop time, decide what you'll do in the first two minutes after it, and see what your overnight numbers say tomorrow. Harvee won't tell you which headline got to you. It can show how your body handled the evening, so you're not guessing.
