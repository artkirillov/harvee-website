Here's a strange fact about normal HRV by age. In one large Dutch cohort, the top of the "normal" range for people in their late 50s sat above the *average* for people in their early 20s ([Tegegne et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734556/)).

Yes, HRV falls with age. But the tidy ladder of "good numbers by decade" that most people imagine doesn't exist. Here's what the research shows, and what to measure yourself against instead.

## What does normal HRV by age actually look like?

HRV is highest in youth and drifts down from there. It doesn't fall in a straight line ([Sammito et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11333334/)). In the Lifelines cohort, which analysed 10-second resting ECGs from 84,772 people, median RMSSD fell steadily from the teens until about age 60, then levelled off ([Tegegne et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734556/)).

Longer 24-hour recordings agree on the direction. The shape depends on the metric: RMSSD dropped to about 47% of its teenage level by the sixth decade, then stabilised ([Umetani et al., 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9502641/)).

Here's what the Lifelines numbers look like. Values are RMSSD in milliseconds: the median, with the 2nd to 98th percentile range in brackets.

| Age | Women | Men |
|---|---|---|
| 20–24 | 52.1 (11.3–205.5) | 47.6 (9.6–174.0) |
| 35–39 | 37.9 (10.8–141.9) | 32.8 (8.6–123.8) |
| 45–49 | 29.2 (8.3–109.5) | 26.0 (7.1–95.5) |
| 55–59 | 22.5 (6.7–83.6) | 21.0 (5.5–89.5) |

*Source: [Tegegne et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734556/). Measured on a 10-second resting ECG, not a watch.*

Two things stand out.

**The medians fall fast.** From the early twenties to the late fifties, they drop to less than half.

**The ranges overlap a lot.** The 98th percentile for people in their late 50s (about 84–90 ms) is higher than the median for people in their early 20s (about 48–52 ms). And the lowest 2% of people in their early 20s (around 10 ms) sit below the median for people in their late 50s (around 21–23 ms).

Sex plays a part too. Women tend to run higher than men in younger adulthood, and the gap narrows around 50 ([Sammito et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11333334/)).

## Why is "normal" so wide?

Look at men aged 20–24 in the table: 9.6 to 174.0 ms. That's roughly an 18-fold spread between healthy-range people the same age. Three things stack up.

**Biology you didn't choose.** In a twin study of 772 healthy twins and siblings, genes accounted for roughly a third to nearly half of the variation in ambulatory HRV ([Kupper et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.CIR.0000146334.96820.6E)). Some people are simply wired for a bigger number.

**Life, day to day.** A 2024 review sorts HRV's influences into four groups: fixed physiology (age, sex, ethnicity), disease, lifestyle, and outside factors. It also notes that HRV rises overnight and drops through the morning ([Sammito et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11333334/)). Everyday things push it around too, from [a few drinks](/blog/how-alcohol-affects-your-hrv) to a poor night.

**How it's measured.** A systematic review of 44 studies and 21,438 healthy adults found that reported short-term HRV values varied widely, partly because of methodological differences ([Nunan et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20663071/)). A 2025 review of 58 studies concluded there are still no generally accepted normal values ([Brozat et al., 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2308-3425/12/6/214)).

So even the table above is one cohort, one method. Treat it as a picture of the pattern, not a pass mark.

## Why does the comparison trap backfire?

An age chart answers one question: how do you rank among strangers? Your nervous system doesn't run on rank. Three problems follow.

**Wrong ruler.** Norms from 24-hour, short-term and ultra-short recordings aren't interchangeable ([Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5624990/)). The table above came from a clinic-style ECG. Your watch uses a different sensor, a different calculation and often a different time of day. We unpack that in [what your wearable's HRV number actually means](/blog/what-your-wearable-s-hrv-number-actually-means). The short version: your watch number doesn't belong on a chart built from someone else's method.

**Higher isn't always better.** Shaffer and Ginsberg also note that certain cardiac conduction abnormalities can raise HRV readings. A chart that treats "bigger" as "winning" misses that. If a reading ever looks odd or worries you, ask a clinician. Harvee is not a medical device.

**A rank with no next step.** "Below average for your age" tells you nothing about today. It can, however, make you feel worse. For some people, constant checking becomes a stressor of its own. We cover that in [when health tracking becomes health anxiety](/blog/when-health-tracking-becomes-health-anxiety).

The better question isn't "Is my HRV good for my age?" It's "Is my body more strained than usual, for me?"

## What should you compare your HRV to instead?

Yourself. A single day's HRV is noisy. Researchers working with elite endurance athletes recommend sensible averaging and long-term monitoring to learn each person's "individual HRV fingerprint" ([Plews et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0071-8)). That work was done in elite athletes, but the logic travels: your own baseline is the only ruler built for your body.

Here's how to build one:

1. **Pick one device and one source.** Switching mid-way resets the ruler.
2. **Measure under similar conditions.** Overnight or first thing in the morning works well.
3. **Give it a few weeks.** You're looking for your usual range, not one magic number.
4. **Read the trend, not the night.** A week's average beats a single reading.
5. **Change one thing at a time.** If your HRV shifts after you cut late drinks or add a walk, you've found a clue.

Once you have a baseline, the shapes matter. Here are [the five HRV trend shapes](/blog/how-to-read-between-the-beats-understanding-hrv-patterns) and what they tend to mean. A dip below your usual range can show up before you feel stressed ([why your HRV drops before you feel stressed](/blog/why-your-hrv-drops-before-you-feel-stressed)), and the levers for nudging it back are in [how to improve your recovery using HRV](/blog/how-to-improve-your-recovery-using-hrv).

**Try this today:** open your last two weeks of HRV and note the range it usually sits in. That range is your normal. Ignore the chart.
